## 1. Goal (one sentence)

After this lesson, the child can map a real argument — what it claims, what it offers as proof, how the reasoning connects them, and where it's persuading rather than proving — using a Federalist paper as the specimen.

## 2. Sequence (teach → practice → comprehension)

| Step | Pattern | Minutes | Activity | Goji tool | Media |
|------|---------|---------|----------|-----------|-------|
| 1 | Hook (discussion) | 5 | "Someone wants to convince you of something important, in writing, and they're *good at it*. How do you read that differently than a story?" Land on: you read it with your guard up — admiring the craft while auditing the proof. That double stance is the lesson | (off-device) | — |
| 2 | **Teach** (first run only) | 6 | Set up the four-box argument map in notebooks: **Claim** (what must I believe when this ends), **Evidence** (facts/examples offered), **Reasoning** (how evidence is supposed to prove claim), **Rhetoric** (moves aimed at my feelings, not my logic — flag, don't condemn). Rule of the map: everything gets a paragraph/section reference | notebooks | — |
| 3 | **Practice** | 22 | Read one selection (Federalist No. 10 recommended first — factions) in the reader, one pass straight through, then a mapping pass filling the four boxes. The Rhetoric box is the fun one: "dangerous vice", "mortal diseases" — name what the language is *doing* to the reader | reader (Federalist — needs ingest), notebooks | — |
| 4 | **Comprehension** | 10 | Child walks the parent through the map, text closed; parent audits with three questions: "Would the claim survive if this evidence were wrong?" · "Where's the weakest link in the reasoning?" · "Which rhetorical move worked on *you*?" Journal (2–3 sentences): the strongest and weakest part of the argument, child's honest verdict | notebooks, journal | — |

## 4. Checks

- **Formative:** the Claim box, checked mid-practice — if it holds a topic ("this is about factions") instead of a claim ("a large republic controls factions better than a small one"), stop and fix it before the other boxes; everything downstream inherits the error. Same topic-vs-claim test as `lesson.reading.68.comp.01`, sharper text.
- **Summative:** parent sign-off on argument maps for **two** founding-era texts (per OBJECTIVES.md), each surviving the three audit questions with paragraph refs; journal verdicts show a real judgment, not a shrug.
- **If not yet:** Evidence and Reasoning boxes blur together → drill the distinction on one paragraph: "the fact is __; the *because* connecting it to the claim is __." Rhetoric box empty → read one passage aloud with full oratory and ask what it was trying to make them feel — 18th-century rhetoric hides at silent-reading speed. Prose itself is the wall → shrink to a single famous paragraph and map just that; older prose stamina is built in short reps, not long slogs.
